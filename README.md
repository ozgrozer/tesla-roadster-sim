# Tesla Roadster Sim

A Three.js stunt simulation of the Oct 15, 2026 Roadster reveal at McGregor, Texas, and a free-roam Los Angeles to drive it in, GTA V-style. It opens in the city, where Optimus walks up to the parked Roadster, the driver's butterfly door swings up, and it gets in and takes the yoke (any drive key skips this); it stays at the wheel from then on. Press **Test site** at the top (or `C`) for the reveal, where the autopilot runs the show until you press any drive key to take over:

1. **The wall.** From the flat start of the platform, climb 21 m and hang upside down at 170° on the 2 G suction fan.
2. **Reverse.** Back down the wall under brake control and stop on the platform where it started.
3. **Fly.** Full power up the wall and over the top without slowing, rear thrusters along the lip, then a push-off with the bottom ones. Roll upright and free-fall with the thrusters idle, then fire full bottom thrust a few metres off the floor (about 3 G) and settle through the vapor cloud. No flips, as at the event.
4. **The lineup.** A long run down the pad on the main thrusters to 180 km/h, then full lift thrust over the parked Cybercab, Cybertruck, Semi, Model Y and Model 3 with a kickflip (a 360° roll, as at the event), the same last-moment landing burn, then brake to a stop.

**The city** is Los Angeles, as GTA V's is: a 10 × 10 block grid between the mountains and the sea, where the Roadster starts in Santa Monica on Wilshire, heading east at five in the afternoon, in the low gold sun. It is low and spread out, with its towers in two clusters: Downtown's skyline on Wilshire (a stepped cylinder with a lit glass crown and a helipad on top, a sail-topped glass tower with a spire, black slabs, flat roofs with big H-marked helipads, crowns of lit glass at night) among the older stone and brick blocks of the historic core, and Century City's glass towers on their plazas round a pair of three-sided towers. Round them, one to four storeys: Santa Monica's stucco and Art Deco over shops, its hotels' names in neon on their roofs; Hollywood's strip of shops with billboards on their roofs and neon blade signs, two theatres with lit marquees, a motel under its sign, the round tower of stacked floors by Hollywood and Vine and the Walk of Fame's stars in the pavement; Mid-City's strip malls behind their car parks, gas stations under their canopies, fast-food signs on poles, a donut stand with a giant donut on its roof, billboards over the car parks and power lines strung along the side streets; Beverly Hills' white houses under red tile roofs on lawns behind tall hedges, pools out the back; Venice's bungalows painted bright. Tall, thin fan palms, shaggy with dead fronds or trimmed to a collar, line the boulevards and every street in Beverly Hills and sway in the wind (jacarandas flower purple in Beverly Hills and Venice, and the street trees are broad and leafy), cobra-head street lights lean over the road, the lane lines are Botts' dots on the low-rise streets (they drum under the tyres), and the street being crossed is named on a lit sign under every signal's arm. Every shop is its own: a sign over it, its frame, door and stall riser, an awning over some, and through the glass a café, a clothes shop, a store, a salon or a bar traced inside (or an empty unit papered over, FOR LEASE); the glass mirrors the street at a glancing angle; at dusk the signs come on one by one, some shops shut later in the evening, and the lit ones throw their light on the pavement. Past the ring road's west side the beach runs along the Pacific, and from the end of Wilshire Santa Monica's pier runs out over the water on rows of timber piles, under an arch with the town's name: drive straight onto its planks (they drum under the tyres) past the carousel's hall and the kiosks to the restaurant at the end, with the amusement park beside you, its Ferris wheel turning slowly and the coaster's train racing round. To the north the Hollywood Hills rise behind a retaining wall, houses stacked on stilts up their lower slopes among the trees, the observatory on its spur and LOS ANGELES in white letters on the slope at the head of La Brea; the San Gabriels stand behind them. East and south the city goes on behind a freeway's sound wall (you hear its roar there): the freeway on its columns ten metres up, crawling out of town and flowing in, climbing over the other in a stack at the corner, and beyond it low stucco and palms to the horizon, fading into the haze. The sun goes down over the Pacific in gold and the smog lies brown low round the horizon. Every window is its own: set back in its opening (at an angle you see the reveal, the sill lit and the head in shadow) with a room behind it, a floor, a ceiling, walls and a desk or cabinet against the back, lit by its lamp or dark; warm or cool, blinds up or down, and the glass smooth enough to mirror the sky at a glancing angle, each pane at a slightly different one. The streets are worn the way a city's are: darker, polished wheel tracks down every lane, oil drips between them, patches and tar-sealed cracks, a concrete gutter along every kerb that follows it round the corners, drain grates by the corners, manholes out in the lanes, and turn arrows before each stop line showing the moves the lane allows. The low sun throws the whole city's shadows, not just those round the car: a tower's runs two hundred metres down the avenue, so the streets lie mostly in cool shade with shafts of warm sun between the blocks, and every car sits on its own soft shadow. People walk the pavements, a metre or three in from the kerb, each in their own clothes and build; at the corners some wait at the kerb for the walk (the first seconds of the cross street's green, so they are over before it changes) and cross on the stripes, stepping round a car stopped across them, and traffic and Autopilot stop for anyone in the road. Drive at them and they dive out of the way, arms flying up, then hurry back to the pavement: nobody is ever run down. Two lanes each way, a signal on every corner, and live traffic (sedans, hatchbacks, SUVs, cabs, pickups, vans, buses, police SUVs and sports coupes, described below, the fronts steering through the turns and the bodies dipping under braking, squatting as they pull away and leaning out of the corners) that keeps its distance (minding the whole of a car crossing in front, not just its middle), signals and changes lanes, stops for red lights and gives way when turning left, taking a gap of four and a half seconds in the oncoming traffic, or going as the light changes and the oncoming traffic stops. Drivers honk at a Roadster that sits in their way or cuts them up, lean on the horn after you hit them (hazards blinking), and move over when you honk at them from behind, though nobody honks until the Roadster has sounded its own horn at the gridlock: the first horn in town is yours. The kerb is felt: each axle thumps up onto the pavement and drops back off it (the car rides up onto it, front wheels first), a wheel over a manhole clunks (ta-dunk, front then rear), and the tyres grip a little less on the pier's planks, less on a park's or a front lawn's grass and less still on the beach, where the sand drags at them and sprays up behind. Driving well scores too, on the stunt score with the flips and landings: a drift, held well sideways at speed, pays for how long it lasted and how far sideways it went once the car straightens up (nothing, if it ends in a crash), and a near miss, flashing past a car close enough to touch without touching it, pays for how fast you went by, double if it was coming the other way. Hit a car and it gets knocked loose; like GTA, nothing on the road takes the wheel away, however hard you hit it: the Roadster bounces off cars, walls and posts and drives on. Landings don't either: however hard it comes down, anything short of its side or roof lands on its wheels, and flying into the side of a building bounces it off. Only coming down on its side or roof tumbles it. Fly up onto a building and you can land and drive on its roof: the stair housings and air-handling units are solid, but the edge is only a low lip, so you can drive straight off a tall roof and come down on a lower one across the alley (or on the street, if you overshoot). Street furniture breaks the way it would: a street light is a breakaway pole that folds over at a walking pace, a signal mast only goes down in a hit at nearly 50 km/h, and trees and palms snap by the size of their trunk. Each one costs the car the energy it took to snap and the momentum of what it carries along, then falls over like a pole hinged at its foot, the signal going dark and a tree shedding its leaves; a car you knock sliding takes them down too. The thrusters work here too, and one block of the central avenue, straight ahead of where you arrive, is gridlocked solid both ways for jumping (the gold blip on the radar). Press `P` in the city for **Autopilot**: the Roadster drives itself to that gridlock on a green wave (every light on its way turns green for it, after the cross street's yellow, and goes back to its cycle once it is through), pulls up behind the last car in the queue, sits there while the light at the far end of the jammed street turns red, leans on the horn, then lifts straight up on its thrusters, flies down the street over the stopped cars and comes down on the open road beyond. It drives like a Roadster: hard off the line, up to about 120 km/h on the straights, braking late and firmly, and taking corners quicker than traffic does. From there it drives itself through traffic, overtaking slower cars when the other lane is clear and keeping its green wave going (the light at the end of each street turns green for it), and whenever it is stuck in a jam again (not just at a red light) it honks and hops over that too, until you touch a drive key. `C` (or **Test site**) goes back to the reveal. Night falls with the clock (below): a deep blue sky with stars and a full moon rising opposite where the sun went down, the hills black against it and the sea dark under the moon's glitter path, half or more of the windows lit and the shopfronts brighter, every street light on, each throwing its own pool of warm light on the ground under it, the traffic's headlight beams on the road and the Roadster's dipped headlights lighting the street ahead; the neon, the marquees, the billboards and the towers' crowns glow, the hills twinkle, the sign on them is floodlit, the freeway runs in streams of white and red, the Ferris wheel lights up in colours that chase round its rim and shimmer in the sea, and the city's orange glow lies along the horizon everywhere but over the water; the birds go quiet and the crickets start up. (Cars parked in the lots have their lights off, day or night.)

**The day and the weather.** The city's clock runs as GTA V's does, a minute every two seconds, from five in the afternoon: the sun crosses the sky as it does over Los Angeles in October, rising in the east-south-east at five past seven, standing 45° up in the south at noon and going down into the Pacific's haze in the west-south-west at a quarter past six, the city's shadows swinging round with it. The light follows: noon's white sun in a clear blue sky over a pale brown haze, the golden hour's low gold sun, the sun going down red into a pink and violet sky, the blue hour, and night under a full moon rising opposite the sun, the city lighting up window by window through the dusk, then the street lights, the signs, the drivers' headlights. Press `L` and the clock runs on fast to the next time of day (morning, midday, the golden hour, sunset, night), the sky and the shadows sweeping round in three seconds. The weather moves on by itself as Los Angeles' does, mostly clear, now and then cloudy (white cumulus drifting on the wind), overcast, the marine layer's fog rolling in thick and grey, rain, and rarely a thunderstorm; `O` brings in the next one (and after the last, back to the weather as it comes). The cloud comes in first, then the rain; the streets soak dark and mirror the city, its buildings, signs and lights stretched long down the wet asphalt (the scene drawn again from below the road, blurred as rough asphalt blurs it), puddles fill in the dips and run along the gutters and the rain rings them, and once it stops the road dries slowly in the sun. Rain falls through a box of air round you, slanting in at the windscreen at speed, glittering in the street lights and the headlights' beams (which you see in the air in rain and fog at night), splashing on the road and the pavement. The tyres throw up spray, a sheet of water through a puddle, and grip about a quarter less: from 100 km/h the brakes stop the car in about 42 m wet instead of 34, the e-brake in 39 instead of 30, and it corners at about 0.85 g instead of 1.3; a sliding tyre hardly smokes. The traffic's tyres spray too. In a storm lightning flickers through the clouds and lights the street, and the thunder follows as late as the strike is far, cracking close by, rolling far off. You hear the rain hiss all round, drumming on the canopy from the driver's seat, the tyres swishing through the water, the far traffic's hiss off the wet road and the wind howling down the streets. At night each street light throws its own pool on the ground under it, bright on the pavement and the paint and dark on the asphalt, with darker road between one and the next (knock a light down and its pool goes out), and lights the Roadster as it drives under it. Overhead, an airliner goes over now and then on its way down to LAX, its strobes blinking at night, and a helicopter circles some part of town for a while, its rotor thudding, at night its searchlight sweeping the streets under it.

**The people in it.** Each driver in the city is their own: some dawdle, most keep to about the limit and a fifth press on; they follow at their own distance, pull away and brake at their own rate and react at their own speed, so a queue moves off at a green in a wave, each car a moment after the one ahead. Nobody stamps on the pedals without reason: speed changes smoothly, the brakes come on hard only in an emergency, and the bodies dip, squat and lean on their springs with it (a bus slower and softer). Drivers stop a little short of the line and creep up to it, a bold one goes on through an amber they could have stopped for, nobody pulls into a junction with no room to get out of it, and a car turning left waits for a gap and then goes as the light changes. A lane change is signalled first and eased over on a smooth S; quicker drivers overtake a slow car. Come at a car and its driver stamps on the brakes, swerves and leans on the horn; come down from the air in front of them and everyone ahead panics; hit one and it slides to a stop, sits there with its hazards on (its driver honking if you are still about), then steers back into its lane and drives on, while the cars behind move over to get round it. A car you send sliding into another knocks that one loose too. Two cars stuck nose to nose in a junction sort it out, one backing up (reversing lights on) to let the other by. Now and then a police car answers a call, its light bar flashing red and blue and its siren wailing (yelping at the junctions), and comes by: the traffic ahead signals and pulls over to the kerb to let it through, cross traffic holds at the line while it goes over a red at a crawl, the people waiting at the corner stay on the kerb, and if the Roadster sits in its way it flashes its headlights, gives a whoop and goes round. Drivers take it slower in the rain and the fog, leaving longer gaps and braking earlier, and the streets thin out late at night, to a little over half the rush hour's traffic at three in the morning. People walk at their own pace and build, some in pairs, some jogging, some standing about on their phones or chatting; heads turn to follow the Roadster when it goes by loud and fast; now and then someone jaywalks once the traffic is far enough off (it stops for them). Drive on the pavement, crash, land or hit something hard nearby and everyone round about runs screaming along the pavement away from it, then calms down a few seconds later. People take the weather and the hour as Angelenos do. When it starts to rain each minds it at their own point: most put up an umbrella (black most often, some navy or red, a few wide golf umbrellas in two-colour panels and clear bubbles), held up in the right hand; the rest hurry on a little hunched with shorter, quicker steps, hoods up if they wear a hoodie, and a few run for it. Nobody stands about chatting or sits on a wet bench, some wait under a shop's canopy until it stops, and in a downpour half of them have gone indoors. Late at night the streets empty, to a third of the day's crowd at three in the morning, and in the evening Hollywood's and Santa Monica's shopping streets are busier. By day people sit a while on the bus benches, stop to look in a shop window, stand talking in twos and threes, and a few walk a dog on a lead, which trots beside them, sits when they stop, crosses with them and runs when they run. At night the nearest street light lights whoever stands in its pool.

The traffic is today's Los Angeles: midsize sedans with long raked windscreens and fastback rear windows, compact hatchbacks, crossover SUVs with roof rails, full-size pickups with an open bed and chrome bumpers, high-roof delivery vans, yellow hybrid cabs with a checker band and a lit roof sign, city buses in orange, blue or red with their gas tanks under a fairing on the roof and a bike rack on the nose, black-and-white police SUVs (some with their light bars flashing red and blue) and the odd sports coupe. Their bodies swell out below a crisp character line and roll over a full shoulder to a high beltline, under shallow side windows framed in gloss black; big wheels on low-profile tyres fill their arches, on alloys of half a dozen designs in silver, graphite or black (black steel on the police cars, steel on the vans, polished aluminium on the buses). Slim LED headlights and tail lights wrap round the corners, some joined by a light bar across the back, with running lights by day, amber and red side markers, a high stop lamp, indicators in the mirrors, flush door handles and California plates front and back; tail lights brighten as a car brakes and reversing lamps light white as it backs up. The paint is LA's mix, mostly white, black, silver and grey, then dark blue and red and the odd bright colour, each car a solid gloss or a metallic under a hard clear coat, some with a black roof. People are dressed for LA: T-shirts and tank tops, hoodies, jeans, shorts, leggings, summer dresses and skirts, caps and sunglasses, trainers and the odd pair of sandals; their hair short, long, in a ponytail, a bun or curls; faces with eyes, brows and a mouth (and a beard on some), skin from pale to deep brown.

**Crashes** leave their mark, as in GTA V. A car crumples where it is hit, the lamps on that side go dark and its glass crazes or falls out; hit hard, it steams from under its bonnet and limps on, and hit harder still it is a wreck that stays where it stops, hazards on and dark smoke rolling off it, while its driver climbs out and walks off along the pavement. The traffic behind edges round it, or, when it blocks both lanes, goes round on the wrong side of the road once the oncoming lane is clear; a wreck left out of sight for a while is towed. The Roadster's own lamps and glass break too, a broken headlight taking its share of the beam with it, until a reset (`R`) mends them.

**The city's own vehicles** come and go among the traffic. A call goes to an ambulance or a fire engine as well as the police: a boxy Type III ambulance flashing red and white, its siren rounder and higher than the police car's and switching to a hi-lo on the open road, or a red pumper with a ladder on its roof, its mechanical siren winding up and coasting down and its air horn blasting at each junction. Box trucks double-park to deliver, signalling, pulling in with their hazards on and running their roller doors up over the boxes inside, then a minute or two later drive on, the traffic going round them meanwhile. In Mid-City and the south an early-'60s lowrider in candy paint rides low on its wire wheels and, stopped at a red, hops on its hydraulics; in Beverly Hills and Hollywood a stretch limousine, black or white, takes its left turns with care.

Fan-made, built from press coverage. Not affiliated with Tesla or SpaceX.

## Run it

**Double-click `index.html`.** It works offline and needs no server: three.js and the fonts are in `vendor/`, and the game code is bundled into one plain script, `dist/roadster.js`. Add `#site` to the address to start at the test site.

A local server still works if you prefer one: `npm start`, or double-click `start.command` on macOS.

### On foot and in other cars (the city)

The city opens as before: Optimus walks up to the Roadster, gets in and you drive. Press **F** (pad `Y`) by the kerb to get out: the gull-wing door goes up, Optimus climbs out and closes it, and the camera eases from the car's follow camera onto a third-person camera behind him.

| Key | On foot |
| --- | --- |
| W A S D | Jog (4.5 m/s) the way the camera faces (left stick on a pad: part way over walks) |
| Shift | Sprint (7 m/s, pad `A`) |
| Caps Lock | Walk (2 m/s) instead of jogging, as GTA's walk toggle |
| R · left click | Punch, soft-locked on the nearest person in front; press again in time for the combo (jab, cross, a hook that knocks them down); someone down is stamped on. An assault is a crime (1 star once reported), a death more; some people fight back, some run, some cower |
| Space | Jump (pad `X`) |
| Mouse | Click the picture to lock the pointer, then look round (right stick on a pad); the wheel zooms |
| F | By a car: get in, as the prompt says (pad `Y`). Walking away from a door cancels it |

What F does by a car depends on the car, and the prompt names it: **Get in** for the Roadster or a car you left, **Break in** for one parked in the street or a lot (a couple of seconds jimmying the lock or smashing the window, and sometimes its alarm goes off), **Carjack** for one with a driver (they are hauled out and run off). Optimus always walks round to the driver's door, opens it and climbs in; out, he climbs out on the kerb side if there is room, or wherever is free. Every other car is taken over from the traffic (`handover.js`) and drives on its handling class's body (`vehicleSim.js`, `handling.js`: gears, weight transfer, handbrake), knocking the city and the traffic about as itself; only the Roadster has thrusters, flight, the cockpit camera and the stunt HUD. Keys held across getting in or out do nothing until pressed again. On foot the HUD is only the radar, the clock and the prompt; in another car the speedometer comes back.

**Every car the player drives looks, sounds and is filmed as its class** (`polish.js` and the modules it runs). The traffic renderer keeps drawing the car as itself (its body type, metallic or solid paint under the clear coat, glass, lamps and dents), posed from its physics body: it pitches under braking, squats pulling away and rolls into corners on the class's springs, its front wheels steer to the body's steering angle, its driven rears spin up in a burnout or stop dead on the handbrake, its brake lamps follow the pedal and its white reversing lamps the reverse gear, and its headlights come on as the light goes. Its tyres lay skid marks and smoke in a slide, a handbrake turn or wheelspin, as the Roadster's do (`render/playerCar.js`), and its damage shows as the traffic's does: crumples, broken lamps, crazed or missing glass, steam then smoke. The follow camera's arm is sized to the car (well back and up behind a van, a bus or a truck), leads a little into turns, opens out and widens its field of view with speed, gives with hard braking and acceleration, jolts subtly with each knock (harder with a heavy hit), and when a wall cuts in it pulls in at once but eases back out. `V` cycles the cameras: Follow, Far, Cockpit (the **Hood** camera in any other car: over the bonnet, or at the windscreen of a van, bus or truck, riding the body), Chase, Side and Free (no Director in the city, where it would only be Follow); on foot `V` sets the camera near, middling or far. Each class has its own synthesised engine (`audio/engine.js`): its firing note from the rpm and gear (four cylinders in a sedan, a V8 in a sports car, a pickup or a cruiser, a diesel's clatter in a truck or a bus), opening up with the throttle, dipping through each gear change and stuttering on the limiter, with the Roadster's electric drive silent meanwhile; the tyres squeal from the body's slip, the horn is the class's (a deeper pair on an SUV or a pickup, an air horn on a bus or a truck), and knocks and crashes are heard from the side they landed on. Getting in, the car's class and name come up bottom right (`SUVs` · **SUV**, `Stolen · Sports` · **Sports car**); the speedometer shows the gear and a rev counter (red at the red line) instead of P R N D and the motors' power; the green bar under the radar is the player's health (the car's harm, or on foot the knocks they have taken); the car's hints are dropped as soon as the player gets out. The cash and the wanted stars sit top right under the top bar, the mission clock top centre (the radio's station drops below it), and the objective over the line of keys, kept between the radar and the speedometer.

Getting in, out, stealing and carjacking are announced on the shared game-event bus (`js/sim/gameEvents.js` emitGame): `car-entered`, `car-exited`, `car-stolen` and `crime` (`{ type: 'carjack' | 'car-alarm', pos, severity }`) for the wanted level to come.

Known limits: the traffic drives round the car you are in, and is knocked aside by the Roadster left at the kerb, cars other than the Roadster leave no skid marks; a car left in a parking lot is drawn on the ground rather than on the lot's slab; a carjack can't be called off once started.

Other keys are in the tables above. W presses the throttle down over a fifth of a second, as a foot does, so a tap creeps the car on in traffic and a launch doesn't jolt. Release it and the car coasts down on regen, like a Tesla. S squeezes the brake pedal rather than stamping on it: the brakes come on over about a third of a second, about how long a real hard stop takes to bite, so a tap takes a few km/h off and holding it stops the car from 100 km/h in about 33 m (a good performance car's distance); held through the stop, it keeps the car on its brakes for half a second, as a driver stops and then selects reverse, before it backs up (pressed again at a standstill, it backs up at once). The brakes are shared out by the load on each axle, as electronic brake-force distribution does, with ABS holding the tyres at their peak: the tyres howl and the HUD shows ABS while it works. Brake hard in a corner at the limit and the stability control lets the light rears off their brakes and regen, so the car runs a touch wide rather than swinging its tail round (the HUD shows ESC). The steering keys ease the wheel over rather than throwing it to full lock: at a standstill full lock comes in a tenth of a second, at 100 km/h in about a third of a second (and no slower than four tenths however fast the car goes, as the steering's reach is small up there), so at speed a quick tap is a small correction (a lane's worth over three seconds), holding the key a lane change and then a turn at the tyres' limit; let go and the wheel comes back two and a half times as fast (with the handbrake pulled, or the car sliding, the keys are as quick as at a standstill). Underneath, the steering is a rack turned at the speed of a driver's hands (full lock in about a third of a second) that comes back to the middle on its own, quicker the faster the car rolls. Each tyre builds grip with its slip angle up to a peak, about 11° on the fronts, and gives less past it, down to the 80% a sliding tyre keeps; the steering stops just short of that peak, so the car corners at up to about 1.3 g, the grip of its tyres. Hold W through a turn, as anyone does on a keyboard, and once the wheel has been held over for a moment the stability control takes the motors' power away while it asks the fronts for more than they can grip, as a Tesla's does when it understeers: the car comes round at the tyres' limit and holds its pace (a junction at 50 km/h on a 12 m arc) instead of piling on speed and running wide, and straighten up and the power comes back. A quick tap of the keys at speed, a lane change, is left alone: the car keeps pulling, its tyres only sharing their grip with the turn. The weight shifts between the axles through the springs, a moment behind the acceleration, as the body pitches. Clip a kerb fast and each axle loses some of its speed climbing it, a wheel meeting it at an angle knocking that end of the car away from it. In a slide the front wheels follow the slide, as a real car's do through their caster, and the keys steer from there: left alone they let the slide run out (with a touch of counter-steer catching the car as it swings further round), the way into the turn holds it and the other way catches it. A tap of Space on full lock swings the car round on its locked rears, GTA-style: pulled with the wheel turned, the rears step straight out rather than waiting for the fronts to drag the car round (about 90° in eight tenths of a second at 50 km/h, a half-second tap at 60 km/h taking it about 90° round, a right-angle turn). Let go and get on the power and it drives off the way it points; keep the wheel turned and it carries on in a power slide. Off the handbrake, a car turning faster than its tyres could carry it round is caught, as a driver's hands would catch it, so a handbrake turn stops swinging soon after Space is let go instead of spinning on round: hold it to a hundred and eighty degrees and let go, and it drives off back the way it came. `W` and `S` together at a crawl are a **burnout**: the fronts hold the car on their brakes while the rears spin against them in a cloud of smoke and a scream of rubber, the tail squatting; turn the wheel and the rears swing out round the fronts in a **donut**, about a turn every three seconds, each full turn scoring (more for every one on top). Let go of `S` and it leaves on its spinning rears. Pulled at speed, Space also puts the car in **Track Mode**, as in a Tesla's Track Mode with its balance set to the rear: traction and stability control stand back, the drive goes to the rears, and the two rear motors share the torque out between them to hold the drift at the angle you ask for. Hold `W` after the flick and the car drifts; steer into the turn (`A` in a left-hand drift) to hold it or take it deeper (about 50°), tap it for a shallower drift, leave the wheel alone and it unwinds and drives on (a small flick is a short slide, a big one a long drift), and lift off or tap the other way and it straightens in a moment, without the tail snapping round. Held in a drift, `W` keeps the pace the drift began at rather than piling on speed, as a drifter feathers the throttle, so the tyres carry the car round the corner instead of out wide: from 50 km/h it comes round a right angle in about 30 m, and held on it circles at a steady pace about 27 m across (`S` mid-drift brings the pace down, and the drift goes on at it). Hold the other way with `W` on and the car swings through straight into a drift that way, a **linked drift**, which scores as one with half as much again for each link. Once the car has run straight again the electronics are back. The follow camera swings round to the outside of a drift, so you see the car side on. Above 100 km/h the active rear wing rises out of the deck on its swan-neck pylons (it stows again below 60), and tips up as an air brake under hard braking. The body pitches and rolls on its springs: it dives about two and a half degrees under full braking, squats as it launches and leans about four degrees at the tyres' limit, settling with a touch of overshoot. Knocks leave their mark, like GTA: anything harder than about 9 km/h dents the panels where it landed, deeper and wider the harder the hit, the metal crumpled and catching the light, and a crash crumples whichever side it lands on; the dents add up (to a limit) until the car is reset. After a crash the wreck stays where it stops, like GTA: on its roof or side, `A` or `D` rolls it back onto its wheels once it has slowed, and once it is on them any drive key carries on, even while it is still sliding (`R` still resets). Keys still held when the car leaves the ground (W for the run-up, A/D) don't fly it until you press them again, so holding W through a jump doesn't pitch the nose down.

The sound is all synthesised, modelled on what makes the real thing. The Roadster's three motors sing with their gear-mesh and electromagnetic orders (a whine locked to the motors' speed, swelling through the housing's resonances, louder under power and changing flank on regen), the two rear motors beat against each other in a corner and stop with the wheels on the e-brake, the inverter's tones step and split apart at walking pace, the gears' lash clunks as the torque reverses, and below 30 km/h it hums its pedestrian warning. The tyres roar with speed and with what is under them (smoother on the pavement with a "ta-dum" at each slab joint, a swish on grass, a hiss of grains on sand; over Botts' dots each tyre knocks on every dome, a dull thud up through the car, so a lane change across a line of them drums at the rate they pass), sing as they near their grip and scream past it, chattering, front and rear each in its own voice; the locked rears of the e-brake scrub and howl, a burnout screams in place with the rear motors revving, and on grass or sand the tyres only scrub. The wind rises steeply with speed, gusting and buffeting. From the follow camera the car is heard outside, late by the time its sound takes to arrive (so it has its doppler on a fly-by); from the cockpit the street is muffled through the canopy. Knocks are layered by how hard and what was hit (a thud, a bumper cracking, steel buckling, glass going; heavier with grit against a wall, a ringing post, a splitting trunk and thrashing leaves), grinding along a wall scrapes for as long as it lasts, and the wreck grinds along on its roof. Traffic is five voices for the nearest cars: petrol engines with automatic gearboxes (idling at the lights, revving as they pull away, shifting up, engine braking), a V8 pickup's burble, diesel vans and buses with their turbos, hybrid taxis, electric cars' whine and warning hum, every car's tyres, with doppler, fading and dulling with distance, and muffled when a building is in the way; the gridlock idles, a car backing up whines in reverse, a startled driver's tyres squeal, and cars knocked into each other crunch where they hit. Horns are taps, double toots or long angry blasts; buses squeal their brakes and let their air brakes go as they stop; a parked car knocked loose sets its alarm off. The city round you: far traffic on either side, wind between the buildings, the surf and gulls by the beach, birds by day and crickets at night, a siren across town, a dog, building work, the murmur and footsteps of people close by, and their screams as they run from a fright. The street canyon's reverb is long and dense between tall buildings and thin out in the open, and your own sound comes back off the walls either side. Press `.` (or `,` back) for the radio, as in GTA: three stations (Sunset Synth FM, Palm Lo-Fi and Downtown House) whose songs are written as they play, never the same twice: a key, a tempo and a chord progression drawn by chance, the chords voiced close and led smoothly from one to the next, a bassline, an arpeggio and a melody built from a motif and its answer, in an arrangement that builds, drops back and comes in again, the station's chime between songs. Synthwave pumps its pads under the kick and throws its arpeggio across a ping-pong delay, lo-fi swings its drums lazily under an electric piano's seventh chords with tape wow and a record's crackle, house keeps four on the floor under chord stabs and offbeat hats; from the follow camera the radio is heard muffled through the car round it, clearer from the driver's seat.

Smoke is carried by the air rather than flung: thruster vapor, tyre smoke and dust drift on a light breeze, curl and billow in eddies, get dragged along in the car's wake, and are lit by the low sun (they glow at the edges when you look towards it). Hot tyre smoke climbs as it trails behind a slide; the thrusters' cold vapor sinks and spreads across the ground in a cloud.

**The HUD**, in the spirit of GTA V: a clean screen while you drive. There is no top bar or button row: the title, the menu, sound and help buttons, the keys card and the credit all live in the pause menu (the credit at the foot of its Controls tab). Bottom left is GTA's radar, a rounded rectangle turning with the camera and zooming out with speed and height: the streets and blocks, the beach and the ocean on the coast, the hills to the north, traffic as dots (wrecks in amber), the gridlock's gold blip, and blips for the pier and the sign held on the rim when off the map. Over it, the time and the weather's icon (the sun or the moon, cloud, rain, a storm, fog). Under it, the car's condition in green (its dents) and the thrusters' air in blue. Bottom right, where you are: the street (both, at a junction) over the neighbourhood, said as it changes ("Wilshire Blvd / La Cienega Blvd", "Century City"), and over it a compact speedometer in km/h or mph (`U`, remembered), with a power and regen meter, the drive selector (P R N D) and lamps for ABS, ESC, Track Mode and the e-brake that light only while they work. The engineering instruments (the thruster schematic, altitude, pitch, G load, air) come up at the test site, in the air, on the wall or while the thrusters fire. Stunts punch in mid-screen as GTA's titles do (NEAR MISS ×2, DRIFT ×2, DONUT ×3, STUNT BONUS up to INSANE STUNT BONUS, WRECKED), the score sits top right in GTA's money style, and hints wait their turn in a help box top left. The loading screen is a golden-hour title card. The follow camera pulls in rather than sit inside a building, or a bus or a van that comes between it and the car (in at once, back out over a moment once it has gone), and when a knock spins the car or sends it sliding, it follows where the car is going rather than whirl round its nose. It gives with the car: it falls back as the car launches and closes in under hard braking, the road trembles up through it at speed (coarser on sand and grass), and past about 150 km/h, or with the main thrusters firing, the edges of the picture streak in towards the car.

**The pause menu**, GTA V's: `Esc` or `P` (`M` opens it on the map; pad **Menu**) stops the game where it is (the car, the traffic and the people stand still, the sound pauses) and lays the menu over the last frame, dimmed and blurred, with the time, the weather and where the car is in its header. `Q` and `E` switch its tabs, `Esc` or Resume closes it. **Map**: the whole city north up, its blocks and streets, the streets' names once zoomed in, the neighbourhoods, the beach, the sea and the pier, the hills with the sign and the observatory, the freeways, the gridlock and the car's arrow; drag to pan, the wheel or a pinch to zoom, and a click or a tap on a street sets a waypoint there (click it again to clear it); from the keyboard the arrows pan, `+` and `-` zoom, `Enter` sets a waypoint under the cross in the middle and `C` finds the car. With a waypoint set, the GPS draws GTA's purple route to it along the lanes (the turns each junction allows, round the gridlock), on the map and on the radar, where the waypoint's blip is held on the rim while it is off the radar; the route is read again the moment the car turns off it, and within 15 m the waypoint is reached and cleared ("Waypoint reached"). With `?debug=1`, `I` then has Autopilot drive there instead of to the gridlock, and pull up at it. **Settings**: km/h or mph, sound, the camera, the time of day, how fast the clock runs (stopped, real time, GTA's minute every two seconds, or four times that), the weather (Auto, Clear, Cloudy, Overcast, Rain, Thunderstorm, Fog) and the graphics quality; the clock's speed, the weather and the quality are remembered. **Controls**: the keys card, with the menu's, the map's and a gamepad's. Over the radar, a clock shows the time of day and an icon for the weather.

**Performance.** The frame is drawn at the screen's full pixel density (up to 2× on a Retina screen) while the GPU keeps up, and at less when it doesn't: the game times the GPU's own work on each frame (where the browser allows it, otherwise it goes by the frame rate, and by how long its own physics, traffic and drawing calls took), steps the resolution down below about 54 frames a second (further at once when frames are well behind) and back up once there is room, so slower graphics get a steady frame rate rather than a stutter, while a slow processor, which fewer pixels wouldn't help, keeps a sharp picture. Every step is checked: one down that doesn't make the frames shorter is undone, so a 50 Hz screen, or a browser holding the page to 30 frames a second (to save battery, or in a frame on another site), doesn't cost sharpness, and one up that doesn't hold goes back to where it was and is tried again later. A change always waits for the start of a frame, so it never flashes black. The city is drawn from what hides the most to what hides the least (the car, the buildings, the street furniture and the traffic, then the pavements, the asphalt, the beach and the sea, and the sky last, only where nothing else is), so the graphics card never shades ground or sky that something stands in front of. The street furniture, cornices and roofs are split into nine districts, so the car's own shadow map, sixty metres across, and the view leave out the districts they can't see. Only the cars and people the camera can see (or whose shadows it can) are drawn, which halves the triangles in a frame; windows too far off to see into skip their rooms, and the wall between them skips the window work; the city's shadows are drawn once into their own map, and read with four taps rather than sixteen; the bloom is worked out at the window's size rather than the screen's pixels, and added in the same last pass that blurs, tone maps and writes the frame; and the canvas doesn't keep a multisampled buffer it would never use. The Roadster's fixed pieces are baked into a few buffers, so it draws in half the calls (and its cabin, whose shadow falls inside its own, stays out of the shadow map). The HUD writes only what has changed, and the city's textures are drawn straight into memory at startup. While the intro plays, every shader the game can need (the city by day and by night, the test site, the thrusters' jets and the speed blur) is compiled ahead, so the first night, the first trip to the test site and the first thruster burst don't stall. Only the cars and people within 45 m of the Roadster are drawn into its shadow map (the map is sixty metres across, so nothing it shows changes), cars further off ride on plainer wheels, and the traffic and the people allocate next to nothing per frame, so the garbage collector has little to do. In the rain the street's reflection draws the scene a second time, at half the frame's size, before the frame (about a millisecond on a fast card: it is timed with the rest, so the resolution steps down for it where it must), and nothing at all once the road is dry; the pause menu's graphics quality makes it smaller (medium) or leaves it out (low), with fewer raindrops and fewer pixels to a point. As the sun moves the whole city's shadow map is drawn again (under a millisecond, about once a second) and the sky's reflections (about once in nine seconds).

Cameras (`V`): **Director** (the autopilot's shots), **Follow** (GTA-style: sits close behind the car's heading, about six metres back and a little above, so the car fills the lower middle of the picture; it opens out a little and looks further down the road as the speed builds, ignores flips; drag with the mouse to look around, scroll to zoom, hold `C` to look behind), **Chase**, **Cockpit** (from the driver's seat, through the bronze tint of the canopy: the yoke turns with the steering, Optimus's hands going round with it, the touchscreen on the dash shows the speed, the drive selector, power and regen and a map of the streets round the car, the view rides on the springs and tilts with the car, and the street is heard muffled through the glass; drag to look around), **Side**, **Free** (orbit).

## Editing the code

The page runs the bundle, so rebuild after changing anything in `js/`:

```
npm run build     # once
npm run watch     # rebuild on every save
```

No dependencies to install: the bundler is a single file with no packages. `npm test` fails if `dist/roadster.js` is out of date.

`npm run og` renders `og-image.png`, the 1200 × 630 link preview, from the game itself in headless Chrome (it needs Chrome and Node 22; set `CHROME` to use another browser binary). The page's Open Graph tags point at the copy on GitHub Pages.

## Tests

```
npm test
```

These run the full autopilot sequence, plus coast-down, braking, e-brake, drift, stability crash, crash-recovery, landing and lineup-bump checks, headless in Node against the same sim code the browser uses. The city tests check that the lane graph joins up, that traffic never runs a red light or drives through another car, that the Roadster bounces off buildings and cars however hard it hits them (on the road or flying), and shunts cars, that Autopilot gets round town cleanly, that no traffic drives into the jammed street and a jump clears it, that Autopilot drives to the gridlock, joins the back of the queue clear of the junction, honks and flies over it without touching anything, that no driver honks before the Roadster has honked at the gridlock, that the Roadster can land on a roof and drive straight off its edge onto a lower one, that signal masts, street lights, trees and palms stand up to a slow knock and snap in a hard one (at a cost in speed, and to a sliding car too), and that random driving never ends in NaN. Street tests check that the kerb runs along each street and round each corner, which surface a point is on, that the tyres grip less and drag more on sand, and that the Roadster feels each axle mount the kerb and each wheel run over a manhole; that the wheels running along a line of Botts' dots knock on every dome and reflector where the ground draws them, in runs of five 0.9 m apart every 7.3 m, that crossing a line is a few knocks and the middle of a lane none, that each wheel strikes each dot it passes once, fast or backing up, and none it flies over, and that the car drives over them exactly as over paint. Camera tests check that holding `Z` (or pressing the pad's right stick in) looks behind the car at once and letting go looks ahead again, without taking the wheel from Autopilot, and that the follow camera never ends up inside a bus parked behind the car, a van crossing behind it or a bus the traffic drives past while the camera looks that way, pulling in once and letting back out smoothly, while a sedan behind the car leaves it where it is. Track Mode tests check that a drift is held for as long as the steering asks, unwinds and hands back to the electronics with the wheel left alone, and ends at once with a tap out of it or lifting off. Drift tests drive it through the keyboard as a player does: that Space with the wheel straight bites at once and stops from 100 km/h in under 31 m, W held or not, while on the turn it swings the car round; that a flick of Space on the turn starts a drift W and the wheel hold steadily from 40 to 130 km/h without spinning, that a held drift keeps its pace and comes round a right angle in a corner's length and circles tight, that holding the turn drifts deeper than tapping it, that lifting off, tapping the other way or steering out off the power straighten it without a snap or a flick the other way, that holding the other way on the power links it into a drift that way which unwinds as gently as the first and scores as one, and that braking mid-drift brings its pace down; input tests that the steering keys ease the wheel over gently at speed and at once with the handbrake. Stunt tests check that a held drift scores once it straightens up (and one into a crash doesn't), and that passing a car close at speed is a near miss while passing it a lane away is not. Pedestrian tests check that people keep to the pavement, cross only on the walk, are never run into by traffic or left stuck halfway over, dive out of the Roadster's way so that nobody is ever under it, and that Autopilot stops for someone in the road. Input tests check that W held through a lift-off doesn't pitch the car into the ground, and the key map (Space the e-brake, F the lift thrusters, H the horn), and that a gamepad's triggers and stick are analog throttle, brake and steering, and a stick held through a lift-off doesn't roll the car, and that the pad stays still cruising (or left alone while the keys drive), buzzes in a handbrake turn and rumbles in a burnout, without cutting a knock's rumble short. Braking tests check that a tap of S is a light brake and holding it a full stop in a real car's distance, that S held through a stop holds the car before it backs up, that braking hard in a corner at the limit at 50, 90 and 120 km/h never slides the tail out past the tyres' peak, that the ABS works only when the brakes ask for more than the tyres have, and that the e-brake bites in a twentieth of a second, holding the rears at their peak with the wheel straight and locking them on the turn. City tests (lots.test.mjs) check that the city is low with its towers Downtown and in Century City, that each neighbourhood is built its own way, that everything built stands on its lot clear of the streets and pavements, that the parked cars have room in their spaces, that helipads are clear of rooftop plant and gas station canopies landable, that the power lines run from pole to pole, and that a tile roof is solid but slides the Roadster off. Edge tests (edge.test.mjs) check that the pier runs out from the end of Wilshire where it is drawn, that the car drives down Wilshire onto the pier and out over the sea on its planks, that the railings keep it on the deck and the sea sends it back to the beach, that the walls stop it north, east and south, and that a car flown into the hills is held up by them and slides back down. Car-looks tests also check that the wheels fill their arches and stay inside the body. Driving tests (handling.test.mjs) hold W and full lock through a turn at 30, 50, 80 and 110 km/h and check the car corners at over 1.1 g on a tight arc without piling on speed, and gets its power back once the wheel is straight, while a quick tap of the keys at speed leaves the power on; that nothing chatters from one physics step to the next (the car's accelerations through a launch, turns on the power, a slalom fast and slow, a corner's exit, a drift and backing up); that a kerb clipped fast costs each axle some speed and knocks the nose away from it, that the climb from the beach onto the promenade strikes too and that slamming square into a kerb costs a jolt rather than most of the speed; and that a car driven off a roof towards a taller building across a narrow alley drops into the alley rather than hanging there. Radio tests write forty songs a station and check they stay in their minor key, voiced close and led smoothly, with the bass in its octaves and a minute and a half to two minutes long. Handling tests check that the e-brake with the wheel straight stops the car about as short as the brakes and straight, W held or not, that a handbrake turn swings the car round and its slide runs out no harder than the tyres can pull (nothing snaps it onto its new heading) before it drives off the way it points, that the steering turns at the speed of hands, that the car corners at the grip of its tyres, and that the tyre curve peaks and falls away to a sliding tyre's grip. One more test checks that the bundle matches the sources. Burnout tests check that W and S together at a crawl hold the car while the rears spin, that steered it spins round on the spot and scores each donut, and that it drives off straight once S is let go. Traffic tests check that drivers differ, that a queue moves off in a wave, that a 300 s run has no car through another, no late red-light run, no instant change of speed, no snap in heading and no car stuck for good, that a car the Roadster comes at brakes hard, swerves and honks, that a knocked car sits with its hazards on and then steers back into its lane, and that the Roadster landing in front makes drivers brake; car-looks tests check that every type has its lamps, where they belong, within its triangle budget; and the people's tests also count jaywalkers, pairs, idlers and joggers, and check that the Roadster on the pavement sends people running, screaming, and that they calm down. Route tests check that a waypoint snaps to the middle of the nearest open street, reachable both ways, that routes between random points on the lanes join up turn by turn, keep off the gridlock's closed lanes, are the shortest the lane graph allows and no more than a few blocks over the distance, that the line drawn for them stays on the road, and that Autopilot drives round the gridlock to a waypoint and pulls up at it. Give-way tests check that Autopilot doesn't wait for ever to turn left across traffic, that people waiting to cross stand clear of the road, that cars pull out round the stopped Roadster and edge round a wreck, that a reset in mid-air sets off no panic, and that a car stopped with its nose over the line is driven round. Weather tests (env.test.mjs) check that the sun rises east-south-east at about 7:05, stands 45° up in the south at noon and sets west-south-west at about 18:15 over Los Angeles in October, that the clock runs a minute every two seconds round midnight into a new day, that the city's lights come on through the dusk and partly in a storm by day, that rain comes in cloud first, then soaks the road, then fills the puddles, and dries after it, that lightning strikes only in a storm, that the weather moves on by itself, mostly clear, and never rains out of a thin sky, and that on a wet road the brakes and the e-brake take 12 to 45% longer to stop the car from 100 km/h (and the e-brake still under 45 m). The people's weather tests (footfall.test.mjs, linger.test.mjs) check that the umbrellas go up by degrees as the rain comes in and down after it, that fewer are out late at night and in a downpour, and more on Hollywood's and Santa Monica's streets in the evening, a few at a time going indoors out of sight, that some shelter under the canopies, that nobody in the rain leaves the pavement, crosses off the walk or is run into, and that benches are one to a seat, window shoppers stop and walk on, threes talk facing each other and a dog dives clear of the Roadster with its owner. Call tests (calls.test.mjs) check that now and then a police car, an ambulance or a fire engine answers a call, never in the first minute and never sent from where the camera can see, that it drives its run fast and goes over a red only at a crawl once the junction is clear, that the drivers ahead pull over for it, each at their own moment, and pull out again once it has gone by, that the traffic coming up to the junction it crosses holds at the line, that people waiting at the crossing stay on the kerb, that with the Roadster in its way it slows, flashes and whoops, then goes round without touching it, and that once there it switches off and drives on as ordinary traffic. The traffic's weather tests (weather.test.mjs) check that in the rain drivers cruise slower, keep longer gaps, brake more gently and corner slower, that a 300 s storm has no car through another, no red-light run and nobody stuck for good, that the hour decides how many cars are out (fewest about 3:00, the rest parked in the lots), and that after a jump of the clock cars leave the road one at a time, out of sight. Damage tests (damage.test.mjs) check that a hit crumples a car where it lands, the harder the deeper, and breaks the lamps on that side and the glass of that face, that a damaged car drives on slower, that a wreck stays where it stops, smoking, while its driver gets out, and the traffic gets round it, even one across both lanes, that a wreck out of sight is towed, that damage draws nothing from the traffic's or the people's random numbers, and that the Roadster's lamps break on the side it hits, taking their light with them, its glass crazes, and a reset mends both. Vehicle tests (vehicles.test.mjs) check that the city's own vehicles leave the traffic's random sequence as it was, that about two vans in five are box trucks, lowriders mostly in Mid-City and the south and limousines in Beverly Hills and Hollywood, that a box truck double-parks, signals, puts its hazards on and runs its door up, then drives on while the traffic goes round it, that every delivery stop ends and nobody waits long behind one, that an ambulance and a fire engine each drive their run and get there, going over a red only at a crawl, that the calls are shared out among the three, and that about half the lowriders stopped at the lights hop and none on the move.
